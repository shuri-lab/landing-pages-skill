## 1. How TikTok Shop actually pays you

- **The affiliate model:** you post a video with a product link; someone buys through it; the seller pays you a commission. No inventory, no shipping, no customer service.
- **Sales vs. commission vs. take-home.** Sales (GMV) is what the buyer paid and lives in one tab; commission is what you earn and lives in a separate tab. A big sales number is *not* your pay. (Teach the money math with a made-up example so beginners don't confuse the two.)
- **Hot links.** Products with heavy seller ad spend behind them (GMV Max) get views "no matter what." A smaller creator's viral video on a product is a tell that the seller is spending.
- **Negotiate your commission.** Rickey negotiates a higher commission directly with the seller when he's about to run a product hard (he did this on the Lego gun before it blew up).

## 2. The follower gates and posting limits (the rules of the game)

- **1,000 followers = the Pilot Program.** This is the gate to add product links and sell. In the pilot you can post roughly **3 shoppable videos a day** (it used to be ~5 a week; it changed).
- **5,000 followers = unlimited.** Post as many as you want per day.
- **Early unlock:** if a video pops off and you start making real sales while still under 5,000, TikTok will often unlock unlimited early. "You generate sales, they're like, come on, let's unlock this whole thing for you." TikTok makes exceptions when you're making money.
- **Shoppable videos count against the limit;** normal (non-link) videos don't, which is why the growth phase uses normal videos.

**Volume Rickey tells people to hit:**
- Unlimited: **5 a day minimum.** He posts 5, sometimes 7.
- Pilot: max out the ~3 a day. "Put as many as you can and see what's working."
- Earlier framing from his answers: get to 3–5 a day as fast as possible; "do 10 consistently and you'll see a change, but it has to be good quality."
- **Always schedule** them (see §8). Don't post live in the moment.

## 3. Getting your first 1,000 followers (the growth phase)

This is the real barrier for a beginner, because you can't sell until you're there. Rickey's options, in his words:

- **Organic, by niche (his default teaching).** Pick one niche or angle and post normal (non-product) videos about it daily, consistently. Real examples: his **friend who hikes** (nature, trails, deer, filmed it all) hit 1,000 "fairly quick"; his **brother gave camera tips** ("this is how you turn the camera off, how you put it in portrait") and built his following that way.
- **Community mutual-follow.** Once a support community is big enough, everybody follows and engages each other. "That by itself is enough" to get people to 1,000. This is a core reason the Head Start community exists.
- **The selfie plea.** A simple selfie story: "Hey, I'm trying to get to 1,000 followers so I can get into the Pilot Program. Follow me, I'll follow you back, I'll engage your content." TikTok is a helpful community; people root for you and follow.
- **Pretty-girl selfie + trendy song + a paid ad against it.** "Followers go crazy from that."
- **The shortcut: buying a page or followers.** Exists (~$1,000 for a page that's already at ~10,000). ⚠️ **Rickey's cautionary tale:** a girl he knows bought a fresh page for TikTok Shop and *started from scratch* on it, even though her natural page was already getting sales. She lost her momentum and had to break through a cold algorithm again, "who knows what they were doing on that page." Buying can set you back. It's also bannable (see §18). Rickey has *considered* cutting a deal with a page-seller to get buyers a discount, but that's an idea, not a recommendation.

**New account vs. start where you are (a real nuance):**
- Rickey and TJ recommend a **fresh account** for most beginners, because people don't want family and friends seeing their videos, and because the people who know you carry a belief system about you ("oh, you a TikTok guy now?"). TJ's angle: block the people who knew the old you; "you need to change your belief system about you, you need to be a creator." The example: people who've had a plastic-surgery or reinvention journey blocked everyone they knew so the peanut gallery couldn't crush their ego on the days a video flops.
- **But Rickey pushes back honestly:** "If you're worried about where motherfuckers go, you probably won't go far on TikTok Shop." If you already have followers or you're close to 1,000, **just start where you are** — don't throw away momentum. So: new account solves the fear, but the fear itself is something to get over.

## 4. Picking products (the heart of Rickey's method)

### The core metric check (his "final way" to decide)
When he's considering a product, he **swipes up on it** to see the metrics and compares **units sold vs. how many creators are selling it**.
- **Lots of units sold, few creators selling it = gold.** Demand with not enough supply.
- The reverse (e.g., 10,000 creators, 2,000 sales) is a waste of a video.
- His rule of thumb from the voice notes: **~500 creators and ~10,000 sales a month is gold** (about 20 orders per creator). "It doesn't matter how good my video is if nobody wants the product."
- **The exact read (his screen recording, an Old Navy men's waffle Henley):** price $18.89 (30% off $26.99), 4.6★ from 303 reviews, 16.3K sold, creator earns $2.27–$4.16 a sale, "Curated choices" badge, #5 top-selling in Men's Tops, product score 4.5/5, free sample available. Last 30 days: 11.4K orders (up 6.8K), 808 creators (up 528), 29.2K add-to-carts, 1.9% click-through. Last 7 days: 2.4K orders, 645 creators. *That's the panel he's reading.*

### Where he finds products
- **Scrolling the For You page.** Shop videos don't show constantly there, so when one catches his eye (a hat, a jacket), he runs the swipe-up metric check.
- **The Marketplace tab** (the Amazon-like shopping tab at the top of TikTok). Scroll it, look at what's highly rated, check the metrics.
- **Collab invites** — brands that hit him up and send product.
- **Off other creators:**
  - **Big creators** who are making real money and posting a lot: see what they're promoting, check if it resonates, then **do it with a different angle** than theirs.
  - **Small / up-and-coming creators who have a viral video on a product:** this signals the seller has **ad spend behind it**. Come with a *different angle* and you can make it go crazy. **This is how he found the Lego gun** — a smaller creator had a viral video, he saw it, realized he could "go crazy" with it, got his hands on it, negotiated a higher commission, and it blew up.

### What makes a product a winner *for him*
Beyond the metrics, he hunts for:
- **A built-in visual hook / a cool feature he can use on camera.** "How can I use the product to hook you?" This is the single biggest filter.
- **A built-in audience** (see §5).
- **Good quality at a decent price.**
- **A problem it solves — want or need,** and the ability to spin a "want" into a "need" (a toy gun is a want, but "teach your kids firearm safety" makes it a need).

### Product research tools (he mostly trusts his own eye)
- **Kalodata, Daily Virals, SoCo** — known tools. He *doesn't* pay for Kalodata ("why pay $50 a month when my method works"), but says lots of people use these.
- **Hit Finds (HIITFINDS)** — he gets it free via a brand deal. It shows metrics, whether a product is oversaturated, selling / growing / declining, how competitive it is, and the creator count; it scrubs your showcase for higher-commission links; and you can look up other creators' top sellers and best-selling videos.
- Note for us: the *beginner* version of this is "shop your house, run the swipe-up check." The *advanced* tool-driven research is Offer 2 material.

## 6. Hooks (the make-or-break, in detail)

- **Three hook types, and you stack them:** **visual, text, verbal.**
- **Visual is king.** "The best one is a visual, because you're actually using the product and that's what's hooking you." The product does something you can see in the first 1–2 seconds.
- **Text hook** — words on the screen.
- **Verbal hook** — what you say.
- **Stack all three + bake in comment bait.** The toy-gun example, which is a masterclass:
  - *Visual:* shooting the camera.
  - *Text on screen:* "an adult toy your kids will enjoy" (the double meaning makes people stop and comment "adult toy?").
  - *Verbal:* "your kids gonna play with this more than they play with themselves" ("wait, he said play?").
  - "So much going on with the sensory, but I'm still showing you how cool this shit is, and adding stuff that's gonna make them comment."
- **Why comment bait matters:** comments and controversy feed the video to more people. Being opinionated/controversial on purpose ("LeBron is the best basketball player ever" → everyone argues) is a deliberate engagement lever.
- Hooks are **trial and error.** "This time I'm gonna do something crazy for a hook." A hook that works on his page may not work on yours. **Experiment constantly.**

## 7. Content formats and angles

- **Unboxing — his go-to starter.** How he began, still does it. "It's the first video you can actually do. I don't know what to expect either, so we go on this journey together. It's the most organic video you can do." His process: pause and read the brief/instructions **off-camera first** so he doesn't waste time fumbling on camera, then film, then zoom to close-ups.
- **Skits — his strength** (he's an actor). "Super easy for me, and it blends into the algorithm easier because it doesn't seem like an ad." A skit using the product in a real scenario beats "hey guys, I got this thing, buy it."
- **Product in its natural environment.** A lawnmower outside, a toaster in the kitchen, pool stuff at the pool. "Be in the environment where that product will be. It just feels more right."
- **POV on Meta glasses (he claims he pioneered this on TikTok Shop).** A first-person perspective people wonder about ("is this AI?"). He films real in-store reactions: the **knife video** — people said "that's at AutoZone," so he walked into AutoZone (and Walmart, and Jiffy Lube) on the glasses, asked workers if they had it, showed the higher in-store prices, then "this knife is on TikTok Shop for a crazy discount." Sold a ton. Gives a "real perspective."
- **Walking billboard.** He always wears TikTok Shop products when he's out. Someone compliments the jacket, he quietly hits record, acts like he didn't hear ("what'd you say?"), gets them asking "where'd you get it?" on camera. "This is a fucking ad," and he runs it up.
- **Product stacking.** He features a second product in the shot (a cool hat, a jacket) that isn't the money focus. People comment "where'd you get that hat?" He **replies with a new video** for the hat — and that reply video now lives in the comments of the viral video, pulling that traffic to the second product. (The suede LA palm-tree hat is a good example: barely any sales, but he's from LA, styled it up with matching burgundy glasses and jacket so the hat "looks even better.")
- **Reply-with-video** generally: answer a comment with a video; it sits in the original's comments and rides its traffic.
- **Angle levers:** want → need reframes; fear/news angles (he rushed a generator video during power-outage news); holidays (Christmas trees, wrapping paper, toys "about to go crazy"); controversy/opinion; pretty girls in skits ("you look nice in that" → you want the jacket).

## 8. Filming and production (his actual setup)

- **Phone:** iPhone 17 Pro Max. **4K, 60fps.** He shoots on the **front camera** (notes the back camera is even better quality).
- **Why high quality:** it makes products look "crispy, clean," and viewers notice ("what are you shooting on? Is this AI?"). Crisp footage makes the product look better.
- **Not the in-app TikTok camera** — it has a flattering skin filter but lower, grainier quality. He switched to the phone camera and people immediately noticed the jump.
- **Lighting:** ring light, or daytime ("just looks amazing"). 4K 60 eats storage.
- **Editing: Cut AI.** Shoots long (5–6 minutes), drops it into Cut AI, which trims dead space, "ums," and pauses automatically, down to ~1:15–1:30. He can **batch 10 videos** in at once; it cuts them in about a minute; then he rearranges clips or moves a better hook to the front. It was glitchy in beta; it's good now and saves huge time. (CapCut is the alternative.)
- **No captions.** "The product is the caption." He experimented, saw videos convert without captions, and dropped them. A caption can even hurt by signaling "this is an ad."
- **Hashtags: minimal.** TikTok now caps hashtags at **5** (was 30). He used to grind SEO hashtags; once videos converted without them, he mostly stopped. Throws one on only when it clearly makes sense.
- **Scheduling: everything.** The single biggest time-saver. Not scheduling means you stop mid-day, break your presence, fuss with hashtags, and lose time. "Automate everything; it's a weight lifted."
- **Repurposing:** **Scaled Creator** (a website built by a TikTok Shop creator, ~$80/mo) auto-reposts his TikTok Shop video to Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube Shorts and relinks each with the equivalent Amazon link. (Repurpose.io is a similar tool.) *Side note he laughed about: he accidentally paid $800 for the year because he forgot to cancel — "that's how they get you."*
- **Time per video (from his answers):** unboxing 5–10 min; most videos under 10; ~15 min for a non-skit once Cut AI does the first pass; skits are ready as soon as he shoots them. 21 videos a week on the pilot is doable but a lot for a beginner — spread it across days so they don't burn out.

## 9. Getting product to film when you're starting (and broke)

- **Start with what you already own** that's on TikTok Shop — **but run the metric check first.** If it isn't actually selling, don't waste a video on it.
- ⚠️ **Buy-and-return (what Rickey really does):** buy it on Amazon, shoot a few videos, return it; or grab it same-day from a nearby store. **The course teaches the opposite** ("never buy and return") because TikTok bans for it. This is a real gap (see §18).
- **Refundable samples** (you pay, the brand refunds you after you make the sales, e.g., 5 sales in 180 days) → **free samples after your first sale** → **brand invites** later.
- From his answers: make ~$60 work; "look in your house and your closet" — big brands (H&M, Old Navy) are all on there now.

## 10. Selling mechanics — what makes the video actually sell

**His full sell structure, from the real Iron Man mask script (a template):**
1. **Hook** (visual + excitement): "Let's get right to it, baby. Christmas came early." Opens the box, product doing its thing immediately.
2. **Who it's for:** "If you're a fan of the movie, you love cosplaying, dressing up, collecting — this is it."
3. **Emotion / benefit:** "I'm having a blast with this. The ultimate Christmas gift. What kid isn't gonna love this?"
4. **Scarcity / urgency:** "They're running a crazy sale right now, so now's the best time. These are gonna be gone for Christmas."
5. **Features:** "Over 10 voice commands, touch controls, control it via the wireless remote."
6. **Clever CTA to the link:** "Jarvis, tell them where they can get this if they're still available." (The link pops up.)

**Retargeting / running it up (from his answers):**
- **Check your sales every day.** If something sells on day one, hit it again from a different angle with another video.
- **Post your hitters every day until they dry up.** "If I'm selling 50 jackets a day, I post a jacket video every day until it's dry. You keep bleeding it until it's gone."
- **Keep products on the side** — a video can pop months later. (His neck-pillow video sold nothing, then went crazy a year later and he'd given the product back.)
- "I usually sell something from every video."
- **Views aren't sales.** 400–700 views is normal for a Shop video; conversion is what matters, not vanity views.

## 11. The algorithm — Rickey's beliefs, with the honest nuance

- **He firmly believes TikTok rewards consistency.** If you haven't been posting shop videos, even a great video "probably won't go crazy viral." "TikTok's not gonna reward your ass" if you just started. His friend made one good video; "you ain't been in it, TikTok's not gonna reward you."
- **TJ's counter (worth holding):** "Why would TikTok throttle you? They make money when you sell, so why would they not want you to sell more?" Rickey concedes he can't *prove* it's a fact, but from experience "there's a switch, there's a thing." His evidence: he has **hundreds of videos over 5M views** — "how come they're not all hitting at once? There's some type of 'let me give you a little bit now, a little bit later.'"
- **FYP drives the traffic, not your followers.** "All your traffic comes from the For You page." Check any video's metrics and it shows where the majority of views came from.
- **But audience resonance helps a video's initial momentum.** When TJ's Ferrari video flopped, Rickey's read: if content is cohesive with your audience, your audience gives it the first push ("oh, cool") and then it spreads beyond them; if your own audience doesn't give it momentum, it won't travel. (The Ferrari either "didn't fit the audience" or lacked consistency.)
- **You can't predict what hits.** He posts things he's sure will go viral that get nothing, and others pop unexpectedly — sometimes **months or a year later.**
- **Practical takeaway for the course:** post consistently and in volume; don't judge a product or yourself off one video; treat views as data; keep posting through the quiet stretch.

## 12. Account safety and violations (drill from day one)

- **No weight-loss or direct health claims.** This got Rickey an account violation he could never remove. People write "LBS" instead of "pounds"; you get creative ("want those love handles gone?") but stay very cautious, because the rules change.
- **No dupes, look-alikes, or replicas.** He got a copyright strike on look-alike "Fendi" slippers — he never said Fendi, but the logo was close enough for the brand to flag it.
- **Show the product you're promoting.** If you promote a camera but only show footage and never the camera, that's flagged as "inconsistent product promotion."
- **Shop violations run on a point system.** Everyone starts around 200. Post quality content for 30 days and TikTok gives you points to stack. A violation deducts points by severity (a product inconsistency is ~8). Too low = limited visibility; deep red = account done. You can **appeal each Shop violation** (Rickey puts it in ChatGPT to fight it, AI vs. AI), and there's a quiz after a violation that gives a point back and drills the rule.
- **Account (community-guideline) violations are different and far worse** — near-impossible to remove. They're strict on graphic/gross imagery now (close-ups of pus, earwax = "disturbing image").
- **Product safety:** skip weapons and toy guns where restricted; pick categories without restrictions; buy from sellers with a high performance score (required in the starter period); nothing medical; no knockoffs.

## 13. LIVE (his honest take)

- **Rickey basically doesn't go live** — maybe 4 times ever. "You can't make money in your sleep when you're live; as soon as you hit end, your money stops." It's hours of energy (welcoming people, pinning products, demoing, answering, blocking trolls), and the first lives are brutal (nobody adds to cart while you sell your heart out).
- His whole edge is **time freedom:** film, schedule, go about your day. He'd rather make on one video what a live would take 8 hours to match.
- It's fine for high-energy people who like interacting; "to each his own, try it." In the pilot you can do up to ~3 lives a week.
- For the course: it's an *option*, not the path. Don't lead with it.

## 14. Timeline, income, and slow months (proof and honesty)

- **First sale:** "probably the first month." Everyone he's helped got sales pretty quickly.
- **Steady income:** he started in September and was making a steady few thousand a month by January — about **four months** to steady.
- **His Q1:** $20,000–$25,000 a month in Jan/Feb/Mar. It slowed mid-year mostly because he was traveling.

- **Slow months:** evergreen/restockable products keep you afloat (gut cleanse, men's body cream, toothpaste, vitamins, health and beauty — people run out and rebuy). Catch the sale waves, ride the slow months, and use them to experiment. "It's never slow for everybody — someone made $80k in commission in a slow month because they found a product and hit it from a different angle."
- **The honest line for all copy:** results vary; his results are his own; most beginners make their first sales in their first months, not their first week.

## 15. Mindset and positioning (his philosophy)

- **Consistency, persistence, delayed gratification, "put the work in."** Most people fall off, like the gym — a motivating moment, then they quit. A big percentage simply won't do the work. "You posted 100 videos this month, you need one more." The volume is the job (he frames it as ~150 videos a month minimum at 5/day).
- **It has to be fun.** If it isn't fun for you, it shows.
- **"Not giving a fuck" is currency,** alongside authenticity. Example: forget politics — the people who mess with Trump *really* like him; he has enough people who really like him and he doesn't care about the rest. You need enough people who genuinely rock with you.
- **His differentiation:** his authentic voice, humor, and flavor. There are **very few Black affiliates** teaching this — most course-sellers are white, and "if I'm trying to learn from this white dude, their style wouldn't complement me." People buy from the person they vibe with. "It doesn't matter if you're selling the same course as someone else — you add your flavor; people buy because they mess with you and they trust you."
- **Not a guru.** Approach it like a homie, include comedy and fun, don't lecture ("not like this is how you all should be doing everything").
- **Experiment relentlessly.** "The whole platform is ever-changing; you gotta think outside the box."
- **No niche once you're established.** He mixes everything on one shop page and has gone viral across health (ball cream, gut cleanse), toys (the gun), tech (cameras, drones), fashion, knives, and sports/outdoor. "If I like it, I'm promoting it and gonna make it go." (Niche is for *beginners* growing to 1,000; drop it after.)
